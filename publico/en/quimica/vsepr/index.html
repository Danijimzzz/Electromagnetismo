    Lewis structure and formal charges

    In the Lewis structure each bond is a shared pair of electrons and each lone pair is drawn as two dots. To distribute the electrons: add the valence e⁻ (subtracting the charge if it is an anion and adding it if it is a cation), join the terminal atoms to the central one with single bonds, complete the octets of the terminals and the remaining pairs go to the central atom.

    $C_f = V - P - E/2$

    The formal charge Cf is V (valence electrons of the free atom) minus P (electrons in lone pairs) minus E/2 (half the bonding electrons). The best structure is the one with the smallest formal charges and the negative charge on the most electronegative atom. If the double bond can be moved to another identical terminal atom, there is resonance: several equivalent structures whose average is the real molecule, with an intermediate bond order (in NO₃⁻, 4/3). Atoms of period 3 or later can exceed the octet; if you check “limit to the octet” you will see the other structure, the one many books prefer for SO₂ or SO₄²⁻.

    Molecules with several centers

    In ethanol, acetic acid or ethene there are several “central” atoms. The geometry of each depends only on its neighbors and its lone pairs: the C of CH₃ is tetrahedral (sp³), the C of C=O is trigonal planar (sp²), the C of a triple bond is linear (sp) and the O of OH is bent (two lone pairs). A double or triple bond counts as a single domain.

    The VSEPR model

    Valence shell electron pair repulsion (VSEPR) theory says that the electron domains around a central atom place themselves as far from one another as possible. A domain is a bond (single, double or triple), a lone pair or an unpaired electron.

    $"domains" = ("valence e⁻ of the central atom" + "e⁻ contributed by the terminals" − "charge")/2$

    • A terminal H or halogen contributes 1 e⁻; a terminal O or S joined by a double bond contributes 0.
    • Type AXnEm: n bonded atoms (X) and m lone pairs (E); the domains are n + m.
    • A lone pair takes up more room than a bond, and therefore closes the angles: 109.5° → 107° in NH₃ → 104.5° in H₂O.
    • In the trigonal bipyramid, lone pairs go to the equatorial plane; in the octahedron, the second lone pair goes opposite the first.

    Hybridization

    With n domains, the central atom uses n hybrid orbitals: 2 → sp, 3 → sp², 4 → sp³, 5 → sp³d, 6 → sp³d². For 5 or more the atom must be from period 3 or later (expanded octet).

    Polarity

    Each bond has a dipole moment proportional to the electronegativity difference Δχ and points to the more electronegative atom. The molecule is polar if the vector sum of the moments is not zero: that is why CO₂ (linear) and CCl₄ (tetrahedral) are nonpolar, and H₂O and NH₃ polar.

    Limits of this tool

    • The Lewis structure is for main-group elements and molecules with all electrons paired: transition metals and radicals (odd number of electrons, such as NO or NO₂) are rejected with a warning.
    • With one central atom, the surrounding atoms can be H, halogens, O, S or Se. Molecules with several centers are written in condensed form and without rings.
    • The geometry comes from counting electron pairs (VSEPR): the angles are the ideal ones or the typical textbook ones when there are lone pairs (about 107° or 104.5°), not the measured angle of each molecule.

    Frequently asked questions

    How do you predict the geometry of a molecule with VSEPR?

    The electron pairs of the central atom repel each other and place themselves as far apart as possible: 2 pairs, linear (180°); 3, trigonal planar (120°); 4, tetrahedral (109.5°); 5, trigonal bipyramidal; 6, octahedral (90°). Water has 4 pairs, 2 of them lone pairs, and is bent (≈104.5°).

    How do you know the hybridization and whether it is polar?

    4 pairs are sp³, 3 are sp² and 2 are sp. A molecule is polar if the dipoles of its bonds do not cancel: CO₂ (linear) is nonpolar and H₂O (bent) is polar.
