        What a redox reaction is

        In an oxidation-reduction reaction one species loses electrons (it is oxidized, its oxidation number rises) and another gains them (it is reduced, its number falls). The electrons one gives up are those the other gains, and that balance is what has to be squared. For example, in $"MnO"_4^- + "Fe"^(2+) → "Mn"^(2+) + "Fe"^(3+)$ manganese goes from $+7$ to $+2$ and iron from $+2$ to $+3$.

        The ion-electron method

        The reaction is split into two half-reactions, one oxidation and one reduction, and each is balanced separately in four steps:

        1. Balance the element that changes oxidation number. 2. Balance oxygen with H₂O molecules. 3. Balance hydrogen with H⁺ ions. 4. Balance the charge with electrons, which go on the left in the reduction and on the right in the oxidation.

        Then each half-reaction is multiplied by the number that makes the electrons given up and gained equal (the least common multiple), they are added and what remains on both sides is simplified.

        Acidic and basic medium

        In acidic medium the method stays as it is, with H⁺ and H₂O. In basic medium there cannot be free H⁺: on both sides as many OH⁻ are added as there are H⁺, each H⁺ and its OH⁻ form an H₂O, and the surplus water is simplified. That is why the same redox pair gives a different equation depending on the medium: permanganate with iodide, for example, forms Mn²⁺ in acid and MnO₂ in base.

        What to do if something fails

        The tool works with the net ionic reaction. If you write whole salts (KMnO₄, FeSO₄), remove the spectator ions such as K⁺ or SO₄²⁻, which do not change. If a species has several elements without a fixed oxidation number, write it with simple ions. And check that reactants and products include both what is oxidized and what is reduced.

        Cells and standard potentials

        A cell converts a spontaneous redox reaction into electric current: oxidation occurs at the anode (−) and reduction at the cathode (+). Each half-reaction has a standard reduction potential $E^"°"$ (measured against the hydrogen electrode, 0 V). That of the cell is $E^"°"_"cell" = E^"°"_"cathode" − E^"°"_"anode"$ and, if it is positive, the reaction is spontaneous. It is related to the free energy and the equilibrium constant: $ΔG^"°" = −n F E^"°"$ and $ln K = n F E^"°" / (R T)$.

        Nernst equation

        Outside standard conditions, $E = E^"°" − (R T)/(n F) ln Q$, which at 25 °C is $E = E^"°" − (0.0592)/n log Q$. The quotient $Q$ uses the concentrations of the ions and the pressures of the gases; solids and water do not enter. When $Q = K$, $E = 0$ and the cell is dead.

        Electrolysis and Faraday's laws

        In electrolysis a current is applied to force a nonspontaneous reaction. The mass deposited or the amount of gas released is proportional to the charge that passes: $m = M I t / (n F)$, with $F = 96485$ C/mol of electrons. With a current efficiency below 100 % only part of the charge does the desired reaction. In a mixture, at the cathode the species with the higher reduction potential is reduced first and at the anode the one with the lower potential is oxidized first; water also competes.

        Potentials from the CRC Handbook and Atkins, at 25 °C, 1 M and 1 bar. Other books round differently (for example Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺ 0.77 V): results may differ in the last digit.

        Frequently asked questions

        How do you balance a redox reaction by the ion-electron method?

        The half-reactions are separated; atoms other than O and H are balanced, O with H₂O and H with H⁺ (in basic medium OH⁻ is added and simplified); the electrons are equalized and added. For example, MnO₄⁻ + 8 H⁺ + 5 e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4 H₂O.

        What is oxidized and what is reduced?

        The one that loses electrons is oxidized (it is the reducing agent) and the one that gains them is reduced (oxidizing agent). In Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu, zinc is oxidized (gives up 2 e⁻) and copper is reduced.
