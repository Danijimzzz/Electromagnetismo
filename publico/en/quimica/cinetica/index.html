    Reaction rate and order

    For a reaction $aA + bB → products$, the rate is how much the concentration changes per unit time, $v = −{1}/{a} {d[A]}/{dt}$, and the rate law is written $v = k [A]^m [B]^n$. The orders m and n are measured in the laboratory: they need not coincide with the coefficients of the reaction. The overall order is m + n, and the units of k depend on it.

    Watch the coefficient: if the reaction is $2A → products$ and $v = k [A]^n$, A disappears twice as fast, $−{d[A]}/{dt} = 2k [A]^n$, and in the integrated laws $k_A = 2k$ appears. Many books and problems call $k_A$ simply k: you have to check how it is defined.

    Integrated rate laws

    • Order 0: $[A] = [A]_0 − k t$, a straight line of [A] versus t, and $t_{1/2} = [A]_0/(2k)$.
    • Order 1: $ln [A] = ln [A]_0 − k t$, or $[A] = [A]_0 e^{−kt}$; a straight line of ln [A] versus t, and $t_{1/2} = {ln 2}/{k}$, which does not depend on the concentration.
    • Order 2: ${1}/{[A]} = {1}/{[A]_0} + k t$, a straight line of 1/[A] versus t, and $t_{1/2} = {1}/{k [A]_0}$.

    That is why, with a table of concentrations versus time, you test which of the three graphs comes out straight: that one gives the order, and its slope, the constant. If the measurements only go until a little has reacted, the three lines look very similar and R² is not enough to decide.

    The constant also comes from the half-life (in order 1, $k = {ln 2}/{t_{1/2}}$), from the percentage that has reacted in a given time or from any two measurements, solving for it in the integrated law.

    Method of initial rates

    Several experiments are done changing the concentration of one reactant and keeping the others equal. Dividing their rates, k cancels: if doubling [A] multiplies the rate by 4, $4 = 2^m$ and $m = 2$.

    Arrhenius equation

    $k = A e^{−E_a/(RT)}$,   $ln {k_2}/{k_1} = −{E_a}/{R} ({1}/{T_2} − {1}/{T_1})$

    The activation energy Eₐ is the barrier molecules have to overcome when they collide, and A, the frequency factor, has to do with how many collisions there are and with what orientation. On heating, many more molecules have enough energy and k grows very quickly; a catalyst lowers Eₐ. With several temperatures, ln k versus 1/T is a straight line with slope −Eₐ/R and intercept ln A, and it is fitted by least squares.

    Energy diagram and catalysts

    The energy diagram goes from reactants to products through the transition state: the height of the peak above the reactants is Eₐ and the difference between products and reactants, ΔH. The reverse reaction has to climb from the products: $E_{a,inv} = E_a − ΔH$. A catalyst opens another path with a lower peak; if A does not change, $k_{cat}/k = e^{(E_a − E_{a,cat})/(RT)}$. It changes neither ΔH nor the equilibrium constant. For equilibrium and the pH of solutions use pH and titrations.

    Frequently asked questions

    How do you calculate the half-life of a reaction?

    Order 0: t½ = [A]₀/(2k); order 1: t½ = ln 2/k; order 2: t½ = 1/(k·[A]₀). With order 1 and k = 0.0231 min⁻¹, t½ = 0.693 / 0.0231 = 30 min.

    How does temperature affect the rate?

    With the Arrhenius equation, k = A·e^(−Eₐ/RT), and ln(k₂/k₁) = (Eₐ/R)(1/T₁ − 1/T₂). With Eₐ = 50 kJ/mol, raising from 300 K to 310 K multiplies k by e^0.647 ≈ 1.9: it almost doubles.
