              Ways of expressing concentration

              • Mass %: grams of solute per 100 g of solution. ppm: milligrams of solute per kilogram of solution.
              • Molarity M: moles of solute per liter of solution. g/L and % m/v: grams of solute per liter or per 100 mL of solution.
              • Molality m: moles of solute per kilogram of solvent (not of solution). It does not depend on temperature.
              • Mole fraction xs = ns / (ns + nd): it has no units and those of solute and solvent add up to 1.
              • Normality N: equivalents of solute per liter, N = z · M, with z the H⁺ an acid gives up, the OH⁻ of a base or the electrons of a redox reaction.

              How to convert from one to another

              Choose a basis of calculation that makes the given quantity simple: 100 g of solution for mass %, 1 kg of solvent for molality, 1 mol in total for mole fraction or 1 L of solution for molarity. With that amount, get the grams and moles of solute and solvent, and the density (m = ρ V) converts from mass to volume.

              Dilutions

              When solvent is added the amount of solute does not change, so C1 V1 = C2 V2. To prepare a solution from a commercial reagent, calculate the grams of pure solute needed, divide by the strength and the density gives the volume to measure. With concentrated acids, always acid into water and never the other way round.

              Colligative properties

              They depend on how many solute particles there are, not on which ones. With m the molality and i the van 't Hoff factor (the particles each formula unit gives on dissolving):

              • Freezing point depression: ΔTf = i Kf m. Salt water freezes below 0 °C.
              • Boiling point elevation: ΔTb = i Kb m.
              • Osmotic pressure: π = i M R T, with M the molarity and R = 0.082 atm·L/(mol·K).
              • Raoult's law: the vapor pressure of the solvent falls, P = xd P°.

              Conversely, by measuring any of them the molar mass of the solute is obtained. Osmotic pressure is the most sensitive and is used for large molecules such as proteins.

              Solubility and temperature

              The solubility of a solid in water is the maximum mass that dissolves in 100 g at a given temperature, and it almost always grows on heating (KNO₃ rises from 13 to 246 g/100 g between 0 and 100 °C), although in NaCl it changes little and in Ca(OH)₂ or Li₂SO₄ it falls. A saturated solution that is cooled crystallizes the difference between the two solubilities. The data are every 20 °C and a straight line is interpolated between them, so the result is approximate.

              Gases: Henry's law

              At a given temperature, the concentration of a dissolved gas is proportional to its partial pressure: $c = k_H p$. When the temperature rises $k_H$ falls, which is why a warm soda loses its gas and a warm lake has less oxygen. The law fails for gases that react with water.

              Frequently asked questions

              How do you calculate molarity?

              M = moles of solute / liters of solution. With 10 g of NaCl (58.44 g/mol) in 250 mL: n = 0.171 mol and M = 0.171 / 0.25 ≈ 0.68 mol/L. Molality uses kg of solvent, not liters of solution.

              How do you prepare a dilution?

              With C₁V₁ = C₂V₂. To prepare 500 mL of 0.1 M HCl from a 12 M one, V₁ = 0.1 · 500 / 12 ≈ 4.17 mL of concentrated acid, which is topped up with water to 500 mL.
