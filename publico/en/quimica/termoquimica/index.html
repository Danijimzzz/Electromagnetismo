Thermochemistry and Hess's law

    Enthalpy of reaction

    The enthalpy ΔH is the heat a reaction exchanges at constant pressure. If it is negative, the reaction is exothermic and gives off heat; if positive, it is endothermic and absorbs it.

    With the enthalpies of formation

    The standard enthalpy of formation $ΔH_f^"°"$ is that of forming one mole of a substance from its elements in their standard state (which are 0). That of the reaction is that of the products minus that of the reactants, each times its coefficient:

    $ΔH_r^"°" = Σ n_p ΔH_f^"°"("productos") − Σ n_r ΔH_f^"°"("reactivos")$

    Hess's law

    Enthalpy is a state function: only where the reaction starts and ends matters, not the path. That is why the ΔH of a reaction can be obtained by adding others. If a reaction is reversed, its ΔH changes sign; if it is multiplied by a number, its ΔH is multiplied by the same.

    Gibbs free energy

    A reaction is spontaneous when $ΔG < 0$, with $ΔG = ΔH − T ΔS$. If ΔH and ΔS have the same sign, spontaneity depends on the temperature, and at $ΔG = 0$ there remains the equilibrium temperature $T_e = ΔH/ΔS$.

    Entropy and free energy from the equation

    With the standard molar entropies $S^"°"$ from the table, the calculator obtains $ΔS^"°"_r = Σ n S^"°"("products") − Σ n S^"°"("reactants")$, the free energy at the temperature you ask for, $ΔG = ΔH − T ΔS$, and the equilibrium temperature $T_e = ΔH/ΔS$ without you having to type ΔS. At 25 °C it also calculates $ΔG^"°"$ with the free energies of formation and compares it with $ΔH − TΔS$. ΔH and ΔS are assumed not to change with temperature, which is a good approximation over a not-too-large interval.

    Born-Haber cycle

    The lattice energy cannot be measured directly, but it can be calculated with Hess's law: the formation of the salt from its elements is equivalent to subliming the metal, ionizing it, atomizing the nonmetal, giving it the electrons (electron affinity) and forming the crystal lattice with the gaseous ions. Solving for the last step, $ΔH_"latt" = ΔH_f − (ΔH_"sub" + ΔH_"ion" + ΔH_"at" + ΔH_"EA")$. The greater the charge of the ions and the smaller their size, the greater the lattice energy: that is why MgO (3790 kJ/mol) is much more stable than NaCl (787 kJ/mol).

    About the table data

    The enthalpies of formation, entropies and free energies of formation are values from common tables (CRC Handbook, NBS) at 25 °C and 1 bar; those of the elements and ionization energies of the Born-Haber cycle come from the same sources. If your textbook uses others, write yours after a “;” (enthalpy; entropy; free energy) and the calculator will use those.

    Frequently asked questions

    How do you calculate the enthalpy of a reaction?

    ΔH° = Σ n·ΔH_f°(products) − Σ n·ΔH_f°(reactants). For CH₄ + 2 O₂ → CO₂ + 2 H₂O(l): −393.5 + 2(−285.8) − (−74.8) ≈ −890.3 kJ.

    How are Hess's law and Gibbs free energy used?

    With Hess the reactions and their ΔH are added; reversing a reaction changes the sign and multiplying it multiplies its ΔH. With Gibbs, ΔG = ΔH − TΔS and the reaction is spontaneous if ΔG < 0: with ΔH = −100 kJ and ΔS = −0.2 kJ/K at 298 K, ΔG ≈ −40.4 kJ.
