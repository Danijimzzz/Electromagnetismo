Organic nomenclature and 3D isomerism

Type an organic molecule, with chains, a ring (cyclohexane, cyclopentane…) or benzene, and get its IUPAC name, its functional groups, the chiral centers (R/S), the E/Z double bonds, the cis/trans of rings and all its stereoisomers, with the molecule in 3D that you can rotate with the mouse or your finger.

Data

As in class: CH3, CH2, CH, OH, NH2… groups joined with -, = or # (or ≡), and branches in parentheses: CH3-CH(OH)-CH3. The abbreviations COOH, CHO, CONH2 and CN are accepted. Rings are written in SMILES.

It is written in SMILES: each letter is an atom (C, N, O, S, F, Cl, Br, I) and hydrogens are added automatically. = and # are the double and triple bond; branches go in parentheses: CC(C)C is 2-methylpropane, CC(=O)O is ethanoic acid. For chirality use [C@H] or [C@@H] (seen from the first neighbor, the other three go counterclockwise or clockwise) and for E/Z, the marks / and \: C/C=C/C is trans (E), C/C=C\C is cis (Z). Rings are closed with a number: C1CCCCC1 is cyclohexane and benzene is written with a lowercase "c", c1ccccc1 (Cc1ccccc1 is toluene). In a ring, [C@H] and [C@@H] tell whether the substituent is above or below the plane: C[C@H]1CCCC[C@H]1C is cis-1,2-dimethylcyclohexane.

    How a compound is named

    1. Principal group. The one with the highest priority rules: carboxylic acid > ester > amide > nitrile > aldehyde > ketone > alcohol > thiol > amine. The other groups become prefixes (hydroxy, oxo, amino, halogens…).
    2. Main chain. The one containing the most principal groups; if tied, the one with the most double and triple bonds and, after that, the one with the most carbons.
    3. Numbering. From the end that gives the lowest locants: first to the principal group, then to the multiple bonds and finally to the substituents (and, if still tied, to the one that comes first alphabetically).
    4. Name. Substituents in alphabetical order (not counting di-, tri-…), the chain with its "an", "en" or "yn" and the suffix of the principal group: 3-ethyl-2-methylpentane, but-3-en-2-ol, 3-methylbutanoic acid.

    Rings and benzene

    • Ring or chain. The parent compound is the one with more principal groups; if tied, the ring when it has as many carbons as the longest chain or more: methylcyclohexane, but 1-cyclopropylheptane. If the parent is the chain, the ring becomes a substituent (cyclohexyl, phenyl).
    • Ring numbering. Number 1 is the atom with the principal group (cyclohexanol, cyclohexanecarboxylic acid); if there is none, a carbon of the double bond (3-methylcyclohexene) and, after that, the lowest locants for the substituents: 1,2-dimethylcyclohexane.
    • Benzene. With a single substituent no locant is needed (ethylbenzene). The names toluene, phenol, aniline, benzaldehyde and benzoic acid are accepted, and with two substituents we speak of ortho (1,2), meta (1,3) and p- (1,4), although the IUPAC name uses the numbers.
    • Cis and trans. In a ring, two substituents on the same side of the plane are cis and on opposite sides, trans. 1,2-Dimethylcyclohexane has three stereoisomers: the cis (meso form) and the two enantiomers of the trans.

    Cahn-Ingold-Prelog rules (R/S and E/Z)

    The substituents of a carbon are ranked by the atomic number of the atom attached to it; if tied, the set of atoms in the next sphere is compared, and so on until the tie is broken. A double bond counts as two single bonds (the atom at the other end is duplicated).

    • Chiral center (carbon with four different substituents): look with the lowest-priority substituent in the back; if the other three, from highest to lowest, turn clockwise it is R (rectus) and if they turn counterclockwise, S (sinister).
    • Double bond with two different substituents on each carbon: if the highest-priority ones are on the same side it is Z (zusammen) and if they are on opposite sides, E (entgegen).
    • With n chiral centers (and E/Z double bonds) there are at most 2n stereoisomers; if the molecule has a plane of symmetry there are fewer, because meso forms appear (achiral, even though they have chiral centers).

    Types of isomerism

    • Constitutional or structural. Same molecular formula, different connection of atoms. Chain (butane and isobutane), position (propan-1-ol and propan-2-ol) and function (ethanol and methoxyethane).
    • Stereoisomerism. Same order of bonds, different arrangement in space. Geometric (cis/trans, E/Z) and optical: enantiomers are non-superimposable mirror images and rotate the plane of polarized light in opposite directions; diastereomers are not mirror images.

    What it doesn't know yet

    More than one ring in the same molecule (bicyclics, spiro, naphthalene, biphenyl), heterocycles (oxirane, pyridine, furan), secondary and tertiary amines, N-substituted amides, acid halides and substituents with an acyl group.

    Limits of this tool

    • Molecules of C, H, N, O, F, S, Cl, Br and I with chains and, at most, one ring (cycloalkanes, cycloalkenes or benzene), written in SMILES; the condensed formula is only for chains. Two or more rings (bicyclics, naphthalene, biphenyl), heterocycles and other elements are rejected or not named, with a warning.
    • In a ring with two or more substituents the cis/trans is taken from the @ and @@ of the SMILES (with centers on both), not from a drawing; "-ylidene" groups (a double bond between the ring and a chain) are not named yet.
    • The IUPAC name covers the usual cases of chains and substituents; if the molecule goes beyond what it can name, it says so instead of making up a name.
    • The 3D shape is an approximate geometry to see the arrangement of the atoms, not an energy optimization.

    Frequently asked questions

    How do you name an organic compound?

    The principal group is chosen (acid > ester > amide > nitrile > aldehyde > ketone > alcohol > amine), the chain with the most principal groups, multiple bonds and carbons, and it is numbered to give the lowest locants. CH₃–CH(OH)–CH₃ is propan-2-ol and CH₃–CH(CH₃)–CH₃ is 2-methylpropane.

    How do you name a ring or a benzene derivative?

    “cyclo” is put before the alkane: C₆H₁₂ is cyclohexane. The ring is numbered giving 1 to the principal group (cyclohexanol) or, if there is none, to a carbon of the double bond and then to the substituents with the lowest locants: 1,2-dimethylcyclohexane. If the ring has as many carbons as the chain or more, it is the parent; if not, it becomes a substituent (cyclohexyl). From benzene, toluene, phenol, aniline and benzoic acid are accepted, and with two substituents we speak of ortho (1,2), meta (1,3) and p- (1,4): 1,4-dimethylbenzene is p-xylene.

    What are cis and trans in a ring?

    In a ring there is no rotation, so two substituents can lie on the same side of the plane (cis) or on opposite sides (trans). 1,2-Dimethylcyclohexane has three stereoisomers: the cis, which is a meso form (a plane of symmetry, achiral), and the trans, which forms a pair of enantiomers (1R,2R) and (1S,2S).

    How do you know whether a center is R or S, or a double bond E or Z?

    With the Cahn-Ingold-Prelog rules: the substituents are ranked by atomic number. With the lowest priority one at the back, if the other three turn clockwise it is R and if they turn counterclockwise, S. In a double bond, if the highest-priority ones are on the same side it is Z and if not, E. With n chiral centers there are at most 2ⁿ stereoisomers.
