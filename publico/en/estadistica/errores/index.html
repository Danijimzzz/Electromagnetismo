    Propagation with partial derivatives

    If a quantity is calculated as $f(x, y, …)$ from measurements with uncertainties $Δx$, $Δy$…, each contributes its partial derivative times its uncertainty. If the errors are independent they add in quadrature; the linear bound (adding the absolute values) gives the worst possible case and is the one used in many lab manuals:

    $Δf = √(({∂f}/{∂x} Δx)^2 + ({∂f}/{∂y} Δy)^2 + …)$
    $Δf ≤ |{∂f}/{∂x}| Δx + |{∂f}/{∂y}| Δy + …$

    For products and quotients of powers, $f = x^a y^b$, the relative errors are added: ${Δf}/{f} = √((a {Δx}/{x})^2 + (b {Δy}/{y})^2)$. That is why the measurement raised to a high power, like the period in $g = 4π^2 l/T^2$, is the one that weighs most.

    How the result is rounded

    The uncertainty is given with one significant figure, or two if the first is a 1 (0.014 and not 0.01). The value is rounded to the same decimal place as the uncertainty and they are written together with the unit: $g = (9.81 ± 0.05)$ m/s². Writing $9.810652 ± 0.05$ is a mistake: the last digits mean nothing.

    Repeated measurements

    With n measurements of the same thing the mean is taken. The spread is measured with the standard deviation $s$ (dividing by $n − 1$), and the mean differs from the true value by about $s/√n$, the standard error of the mean. It is combined in quadrature with the resolution of the instrument: if the instrument dominates, measuring more times improves nothing.

    Correlated variables

    Quadrature addition only holds if the errors are independent. If two variables rise and fall together (measured with the same badly calibrated instrument, or both coming from the same fit) a cross term with their covariance must be added, $"cov"(x, y) = r · Δx · Δy$, where $r$ is the correlation coefficient, between −1 and 1. The term carries the sign of the derivatives, so it can increase or reduce the error: in a sum with $r > 0$ the errors reinforce each other, and in a difference they cancel.

    $Δf^2 = ({∂f}/{∂x} Δx)^2 + ({∂f}/{∂y} Δy)^2 + 2 {∂f}/{∂x} {∂f}/{∂y} "cov"(x, y)$

    Weighted mean

    When there are several measurements of the same quantity with different uncertainties, they do not all weigh the same: each enters with weight $w_i = 1/σ_i^2$. The uncertainty of the mean is smaller than that of the best measurement. The reduced $χ^2$ tells whether the measurements are compatible with each other: close to 1, they are; if considerably larger, they differ more than their uncertainties say, and here the uncertainty of the mean is enlarged by multiplying it by $√{χ^2_"red"}$.

    $x̄ = {∑ w_i x_i}/{∑ w_i}$,   $σ_{x̄} = 1/√{∑ w_i}$,   $χ^2_"red" = 1/{n − 1} ∑ ({x_i − x̄}/{σ_i})^2$

    Compare with the accepted value

    To know whether a result agrees with the table value, the percentage difference is not enough: the discrepancy must be measured in uncertainties, $z = |x − x_"ac"|/√{Δx^2 + Δx_"ac"^2}$. Up to 1 σ the result is compatible; up to 2 σ, compatible at 2σ (it happens in one in three correct measurements); between 2 and 3 σ it is doubtful, and above 3 σ it is incompatible: there is a systematic error or the uncertainty is underestimated.

    Frequently asked questions

    How does error propagate in a calculated quantity?

    σ_f = √(Σ(∂f/∂xᵢ · σᵢ)²). For f = x·y with x = 10 ± 0.1 and y = 5 ± 0.1, the relative errors add in quadrature: √(0.01² + 0.02²) ≈ 2.2 %, so f = 50 ± 1.1.

    How is a measurement rounded together with its uncertainty?

    The uncertainty is given with one or two significant figures and the value is rounded to the same decimal place. For sums and differences, σ = √(σ₁² + σ₂²).
