Regression and correlation

    Covariance and correlation

    For N pairs (xi, yi) the covariance measures whether the two variables grow together (positive) or one grows while the other falls (negative):

    $σ_{xy} = {∑ x_i y_i}/N − x̄ ȳ$

    It depends on the units, so to measure the strength of the relationship it is divided by the standard deviations, giving Pearson's correlation coefficient, which is always between −1 and 1:

    $r = σ_{xy}/{σ_x σ_y}$

    • r = 1 or r = −1: the points lie exactly on a line.
    • |r| close to 1 (above 0.9): very strong correlation; between 0.7 and 0.9, strong; between 0.5 and 0.7, moderate; below that, weak.
    • r close to 0: there is no linear relationship, although there may be another kind (a parabola, for example).

    Correlation is not causation: two variables may go together because both depend on a third.

    The two regression lines

    The regression line of Y on X is the one that minimizes the sum of the squares of the vertical distances from the points to the line (least squares). It is used to estimate y knowing x:

    $y − ȳ = σ_{xy}/{σ_x^2} (x − x̄)$

    To estimate x knowing y you do not solve that line for x; you use the line of X on Y, which minimizes the horizontal distances:

    $x − x̄ = σ_{xy}/{σ_y^2} (y − ȳ)$

    Both pass through the centroid (x̄, ȳ) and the product of their slopes (the second written as dx/dy) is r2: if |r| = 1 they coincide and if r = 0 they are perpendicular. The coefficient of determination r2 is the share of the variation in y explained by the line. An estimate is reliable if r2 is high and the value lies within the range of the data; outside it, it is an extrapolation.

    Fits that are not straight lines

    Many fits become a straight line by changing variable and are solved the same way:

    • Exponential, $y = a e^{bx}$: taking logarithms, $ln y = ln a + b x$, a line between x and ln y.
    • Power, $y = a x^b$: $ln y = ln a + b ln x$, a line between ln x and ln y.
    • Logarithmic, $y = a + b ln x$: a line between ln x and y.
    • Hyperbolic, $y = a + b/x$: a line between 1/x and y (for example, Boyle's law).
    • Line through the origin, $y = bx$: when theory says y = 0 if x = 0, only $b = {∑ x_i y_i}/{∑ x_i^2}$ is fitted.

    The parabola $y = a + bx + cx^2$ is fitted directly by least squares: a system of three equations (the normal equations) is solved. To compare fits use $R^2 = 1 − {∑ (y_i − ŷ_i)^2}/{∑ (y_i − ȳ)^2}$ on the original y values.

    With N or with N − 1

    In high school the variances and covariance are divided by N; at university, often by N − 1 (sample variances, $s_x^2$ and $s_{xy}$), because they estimate the population ones better. The correlation coefficient and the lines come out the same with either.

    Two-way table

    When there are many repeated data they are given in a table: the x values in the rows, the y values in the columns and in each cell how many times that pair appears. Each cell with a frequency is a point with its weight ni.

    Spearman correlation

    Each value is replaced by its position from smallest to largest (its rank) and r is calculated with the ranks. It equals 1 if y always grows when x grows, even if not in a straight line, and it is less affected by outliers. Without ties, $r_s = 1 − {6 ∑ d_i^2}/{n (n^2 − 1)}$, with $d_i$ the difference of ranks.

    Error of the slope and the intercept

    In lab work each parameter is given with its uncertainty. With $s_e = √({∑ (y_i − ŷ_i)^2}/{N − 2})$ and $S_{xx} = ∑ (x_i − x̄)^2$:

    $s_b = s_e/√S_{xx}$,   $s_a = s_e √(1/N + x̄^2/S_{xx})$

    The error is rounded to one significant figure (two if it starts with 1) and the value to the same decimals: b = 0.0398 ± 0.0008. With Student's t with N − 2 degrees of freedom you get the confidence intervals of the slope, the intercept and an estimate.

    Residuals and outliers

    If the model is appropriate, the residuals $e_i = y_i − ŷ_i$ scatter randomly around 0. If they form a curve, another fit is needed. A point with a residual of more than twice its standard deviation may be a measurement or copying error.

    Is the correlation significant?

    With few data a high r can occur by chance. To test whether the population correlation ρ is different from zero we use

    $t = r √(N − 2)/√(1 − r^2)$

    which, if ρ = 0, follows a Student's t with N − 2 degrees of freedom. If the p-value is less than 0.05 the correlation is significant at 5 %. You can compute the t in Probability distributions.

    Confidence interval for ρ and comparing two correlations

    The coefficient r of a sample estimates the population correlation ρ. Since r is confined between −1 and 1, its distribution is skewed; Fisher's transformation $z = "artanh"(r)$ makes it almost normal, with standard deviation $1/√{N − 3}$. The interval is built on z and the change is undone with the hyperbolic tangent. To compare the correlations of two independent samples, their z values are subtracted:

    $z ± z_{α/2}/√{N − 3}$,   $ρ = "tanh"(z)$,   $Z = {z_1 − z_2}/{√(1/(N_1 − 3) + 1/(N_2 − 3))}$

    Weighted fit: when each y has its own error

    In the lab not all points are measured equally well. If each $y_i$ has its uncertainty $σ_i$, the line is fitted giving each point the weight $w_i = 1/σ_i^2$, that is, minimizing $χ^2 = ∑ ((y_i − a − b x_i)/σ_i)^2$. The errors of a and b then come from the uncertainties of the data, and the reduced $χ^2$ ($χ^2$ divided by $N − 2$) tells whether the line is compatible with them: close to 1, it is; if much larger, either the model is not a line or the uncertainties are underestimated.

    $b = {S S_{xy} − S_x S_y}/Δ$,   $a = {S_{xx} S_y − S_x S_{xy}}/Δ$,   $σ_b = √{S/Δ}$,   $σ_a = √{S_{xx}/Δ}$,   $Δ = S S_{xx} − S_x^2$

    Frequently asked questions

    How is the regression line calculated?

    y = a + bx, with b = Sₓᵧ/Sₓₓ and a = ȳ − b·x̄. With the points (1, 2), (2, 4), (3, 5), (4, 4), (5, 5): x̄ = 3, ȳ = 4, Sₓᵧ = 6, Sₓₓ = 10, so b = 0.6 and a = 2.2.

    How is the correlation coefficient interpreted?

    r = Sₓᵧ/√(SₓₓSᵧᵧ) ranges from −1 to 1, and R² = r² is the share of the variability of y explained by the line. In the previous example, r = 6/√60 ≈ 0.77 and R² = 0.6. Spearman uses ranks instead of values.
