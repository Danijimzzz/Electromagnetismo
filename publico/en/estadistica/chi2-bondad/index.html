χ² goodness of fit and independence, Fisher and McNemar

Check whether some observed frequencies fit a model (a fair die, some proportions, a binomial, Poisson or normal) or whether the two variables of a contingency table are independent, with the expected frequencies, the χ² statistic, the p-value and the working.

    The idea

    The observed frequencies $O_i$ are compared with the expected ones $E_i$ that there would be if the null hypothesis were true. If they are similar, χ² is small; if they differ a lot, it is large and $H_0$ is rejected. The test is always right-tailed.

    $χ^2 = ∑ {(O_i − E_i)^2}/{E_i}$

    Goodness of fit

    $H_0$ is the model: $E_i = N p_i$, with $p_i$ the probability of each class according to the model. The degrees of freedom are $ν = k − 1 − m$, with $k$ classes and $m$ parameters estimated from the data itself (one for the binomial with known n or the Poisson, two for the normal).

    Contingency tables

    $H_0$: the two variables are independent (or, if each row is a different population, all have the same proportions: homogeneity). The calculation is the same in both cases, with $E_{ij} = {F_i · C_j}/N$ (totals of row $i$ and column $j$) and $ν = (r − 1)(c − 1)$. In 2 × 2 tables Yates' correction can be applied, which subtracts 0.5 from each $|O − E|$. Cramér's V, $V = √({χ^2}/{N (min(r, c) − 1)})$, measures the strength of the relationship between 0 and 1.

    Residuals: where the association is

    The χ² says there is an association, but not between which categories. The standardized residual of each cell, $r_{ij} = {O_{ij} − E_{ij}}/√{E_{ij}(1 − F_i/N)(1 − C_j/N)}$, is approximately $N(0, 1)$ under $H_0$: those beyond ±2 (marked with ⚑) are the cells with more or fewer cases than chance would give.

    Fisher's exact test (2 × 2)

    With little data (some expected frequency below 5) the χ² is not valid. Fisher uses no approximations: it fixes the margins of the table and calculates the exact probability of each possible table with the hypergeometric, $P(X = k) = {\binom{F_1}{k}\binom{F_2}{C_1 − k}}/{\binom{N}{C_1}}$. The two-sided p-value adds the tables as unlikely as the observed one or more. It also gives the sample odds ratio $n_{11} n_{22}/(n_{12} n_{21})$.

    McNemar (paired data)

    When the two “groups” are the same people measured before and after (yes/no), only those who change matter: $b$ (from yes to no) and $c$ (from no to yes). $χ^2 = (|b − c| − 1)^2/(b + c)$ with 1 degree of freedom; if $b + c$ is small, the exact p-value is better, which is that of a binomial $B(b + c, 1/2)$.

    When the approximation holds

    The χ² distribution is an approximation that works well when all the expected frequencies are at least 5. If not, neighbouring classes are merged (each class merged removes one degree of freedom).

    Frequently asked questions

    How is a χ² goodness-of-fit test carried out?

    χ² = Σ(O − E)²/E, with k − 1 − (estimated parameters) degrees of freedom. A die rolled 60 times gives 8, 10, 12, 9, 11 and 10; with E = 10 on each face, χ² = 1.0 with 5 degrees of freedom, below the critical value 11.07, and it is not rejected that it is fair.

    And the independence test in a contingency table?

    The expected frequency of each cell is E = (row total · column total)/total, and the degrees of freedom are (rows − 1)(columns − 1). A 2×2 table has 1 degree of freedom and its critical value at 5 % is 3.84.
