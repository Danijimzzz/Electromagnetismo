Matrices step by step

    Gauss elimination

    With three row operations any matrix can be brought to row echelon form: swap two rows, multiply a row by a nonzero number and add to a row a multiple of another. Each row starts with more zeros than the previous one, and the first nonzero entry of each row is its pivot. If in addition each pivot is 1 and the only nonzero entry in its column, the matrix is in reduced row echelon form (Gauss-Jordan).

    The rank is the number of nonzero rows left after row reduction. It equals the number of linearly independent columns (or rows).

    Determinant

    Adding to a row a multiple of another does not change the determinant, swapping two rows changes its sign and multiplying a row by k multiplies it by k. That is why it is enough to make the matrix triangular and multiply the diagonal. For 2×2 and 3×3 the cross-product rule and Sarrus' rule are used, and for larger matrices Laplace expansion along the row or column with the most zeros.

    $|A| = Σ_j (−1)^{i+j} a_ij |A_ij|$

    Inverse

    A square matrix has an inverse if and only if its determinant is not zero. With Gauss-Jordan, [A | I] is reduced until the identity is on the left, and then A−1 is on the right. With cofactors the formula is used:

    $A^{−1} = 1/{|A|} · ("Adj" A)^t$

    Eigenvalues and eigenvectors

    λ is an eigenvalue of A if there is a vector v ≠ 0 with A v = λ v. The eigenvalues are the roots of the characteristic polynomial |A − λI| = 0, and the eigenvectors of each λ are the nonzero solutions of (A − λI) v = 0. The matrix is diagonalizable when each eigenvalue has as many independent eigenvectors as its multiplicity. Then A = P D P−1, with the eigenvectors in the columns of P and the eigenvalues on the diagonal of D. Powers then come out easily: An = P Dn P−1.

    If some eigenvalue lacks eigenvectors, the matrix is not diagonalizable, but it can be brought to its Jordan form J = P−1 A P: almost diagonal, with a 1 above the diagonal in each block. The columns of P form chains of generalized eigenvectors, (A − λI) v2 = v1. If A is real symmetric, it can always be diagonalized with an orthogonal matrix Q (Q−1 = Qt), orthonormalizing the eigenvectors with Gram-Schmidt.

    Kernel, image and subspaces

    The kernel of A is the set of vectors x with A·x = 0 and the image is the set of vectors y that can be written as A·x: the subspace spanned by the columns. Everything comes from the reduced row echelon form: the nonzero rows are the implicit equations of the kernel, the unknowns without a pivot are the parameters of its parametric equations and the columns of A with a pivot are a basis of the image. The implicit equations of the image come from the kernel of the transpose. It always holds that dim Ker A + dim Im A = number of columns (rank–nullity theorem).

    When an eigenvalue is not rational, such as (1 ± √5)/2, its eigenvectors are given to four digits and, for 2 × 2 matrices, also with radicals.

    With a parameter

    When the matrix contains a letter (k, a, m…), the determinant is a polynomial in it. Its roots are the values where A stops having an inverse and the rank drops. For the rank, look for the minor of highest order that is not 0 for almost all values; the rank only drops when all the minors of that order vanish at once, and at each of those values the matrix is substituted and row-reduced.

    Matrix equations and LU

    In A·X = B you solve by multiplying by A−1 on the left, X = A−1·B, and in X·A = B on the right, X = B·A−1: the order matters because matrix multiplication is not commutative. The decomposition A = L·U stores the Gauss elimination: U is the echelon matrix and L, lower triangular with ones on the diagonal, holds the multipliers of each step.

    Frequently asked questions

    How are the determinant and the inverse of a matrix calculated?

    For 2×2, det = ad − bc; for 3×3, Sarrus' rule. The inverse exists if det ≠ 0: A⁻¹ = (1/det)·adj(A)ᵗ. For [[1, 2], [3, 4]], det = 4 − 6 = −2 and A⁻¹ = [[−2, 1], [3/2, −1/2]].

    How are the eigenvalues calculated?

    Solve det(A − λI) = 0. For [[2, 1], [1, 2]]: (2 − λ)² − 1 = 0, that is λ² − 4λ + 3 = 0, with λ = 1 and λ = 3. The rank is found with Gauss's method by counting the nonzero rows.
