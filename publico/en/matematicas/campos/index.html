    Gradient and directional derivative

    The gradient of a scalar field collects its partial derivatives: $∇f = ({∂f}/{∂x}, {∂f}/{∂y}, {∂f}/{∂z})$. It points where f grows fastest, its magnitude is that maximum rate and it is perpendicular to the level curves (or surfaces).

    The derivative in the direction of a vector u is $D_u f = ∇f · {u}/{|u|}$. It is at most $|∇f|$, in the direction of the gradient, and 0 along a level curve.

    The Laplacian is $∇² f = {∂^2 f}/{∂x^2} + {∂^2 f}/{∂y^2} + {∂^2 f}/{∂z^2}$. Functions with Laplacian 0 are called harmonic: the electrostatic potential is one where there are no charges.

    Divergence and curl

    $∇ · F = {∂P}/{∂x} + {∂Q}/{∂y} + {∂R}/{∂z}$

    $∇ × F = ({∂R}/{∂y} − {∂Q}/{∂z}, {∂P}/{∂z} − {∂R}/{∂x}, {∂Q}/{∂x} − {∂P}/{∂y})$

    The divergence measures how much field leaves a point: positive at a source and negative at a sink. The curl measures how much it rotates: a paddle wheel placed in the field turns at half the curl. The field (y, 0) does not go around and yet it has curl, because it pushes harder above than below.

    Conservative fields and line integral

    The work of F along a curve $r(t)$ with $t ∈ [a, b]$ is $W = ∫_C F · dr = ∫_a^b F(r(t)) · r′(t) dt$. If the curve is a polygonal path (the outline of a triangle, for example), the contribution of each segment is added, traversed as $r(t) = A + t (B − A)$ with $t ∈ [0, 1]$.

    If the curl is 0 (in a region without holes), F is conservative: $F = ∇φ$ and the work depends only on the endpoints, $W = φ(B) − φ(A)$. On a closed curve it is 0. The potential is found by integrating one variable at a time: $φ = ∫ P dx + g(y, z)$; differentiating with respect to y and equating to Q gives $g$ up to a function of z, and with R it is finished. For example, for $F = (2xy, x^2 + 3y^2)$: $φ = x^2 y + g(y)$, $x^2 + g′(y) = x^2 + 3y^2$, so $φ = x^2 y + y^3 + K$.

    Integral of a scalar field: mass of a wire

    $∫_C f ds = ∫_a^b f(r(t)) |r′(t)| dt$

    There is no direction here: the values of f are added, weighted by the length of each piece of curve. With $f = 1$ it is the length; if f is the linear density of a wire, it is its mass M, and the centre of mass is $x̄ = {1}/{M} ∫_C x f ds$ (likewise with y and z). A semicircle of radius 1 with density $y$ has mass 2 and its centre of mass at $(0, π/4)$.

    Green's theorem

    $∮_C P dx + Q dy = ∬_D ({∂Q}/{∂x} − {∂P}/{∂y}) dA$

    The circulation around the boundary of a region, traversed counterclockwise, is the double integral of the curl inside. With the outward normal you get the divergence theorem in the plane: $∮_C F · n ds = ∬_D ∇ · F dA$. For example, the area of D is $1/2 ∮_C x dy − y dx$.

    The region can be a polygon (the boundary is its sides, traversed counterclockwise) or the region between two curves $g_1(x) ≤ y ≤ g_2(x)$ with $a ≤ x ≤ b$, where the double integral is done as an iterated one: $∫_a^b ∫_{g_1(x)}^{g_2(x)} … dy dx$. If the field has a singularity inside D (like $(−y, x)/(x^2 + y^2)$ at the origin), Green's theorem cannot be applied.

    Flux through a surface and Stokes' theorem

    $Φ = ∬_S F · dS = ∬ F(r(u, v)) · (r_u × r_v) du dv$

    With the surface given as $r(u, v)$, the cross product $r_u × r_v$ is a normal vector and its magnitude is the area element. For example, the sphere of radius R is $r = (R sin u cos v, R sin u sin v, R cos u)$ with $u ∈ [0, π]$ and $v ∈ [0, 2π]$, and its normal points outward.

    $∬_S (∇ × F) · dS = ∮_{∂S} F · dr$

    Stokes' theorem says that the flux of the curl through a surface is the circulation around its boundary, traversed leaving the surface on the left when seen from the tip of the normal. All surfaces with the same boundary give the same result, and on a closed surface (without boundary) it is 0. Green is the planar case.

    Divergence theorem (Gauss)

    $∯_S F · dS = ∭_V ∇ · F dV$

    The outward flux through a closed surface is the integral of the divergence inside. For a box the faces are added one by one (on the face $x = x_2$ the flux is $∬ P dy dz$; on $x = x_1$, $−∬ P dy dz$); for a sphere or a cylinder, spherical or cylindrical coordinates are used. If the divergence is constant, the flux is it times the volume.

    In electromagnetism, the electric field is $E = −∇V$ and Gauss's law is the divergence theorem: the field of a point charge has divergence 0 outside it, but its flux through a sphere surrounding it is $4π$ (times $kq$), because there is a singularity inside; you can see it in the electrostatics simulator.

    Frequently asked questions

    How do you compute the gradient and the directional derivative?

    ∇f = (fₓ, f_y, f_z) and D_u f = ∇f · u, with u a unit vector. For f = x²y at (1, 2), ∇f = (2xy, x²) = (4, 1); in the direction (3, 4)/5, D_u f = (12 + 4)/5 = 3.2.

    How do you compute the divergence and the curl?

    div F = ∂Fₓ/∂x + ∂F_y/∂y + ∂F_z/∂z and curl F = ∇ × F. For F = (x, y, z), div F = 3 and curl F = 0 (it is conservative). For F = (−y, x, 0), div F = 0 and curl F = (0, 0, 2).
