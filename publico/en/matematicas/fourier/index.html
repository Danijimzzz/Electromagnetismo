Fourier series

Write a piecewise periodic function (or a function on [0, L] to expand it in sines or cosines) and get a₀, aₙ and bₙ with their general term, the parity, what happens at jumps and Parseval's identity. The graph adds the harmonics one by one.

Data

Each piece goes from one endpoint to the next: −π ≤ x < 0, 0 ≤ x < π. If you leave a gap, f = 0 is taken there. Write the functions with x: 1, x, x^2, |x|, sin x, π − x, e^x…

    The Fourier series

    A periodic function of period T (with fundamental frequency $ω_0 = 2π/T$) can be written as a sum of sines and cosines of frequencies that are multiples of $ω_0$:

    $f(x) ∼ {a_0}/{2} + Σ_{n=1}^{∞} (a_n cos(n ω_0 x) + b_n sen(n ω_0 x))$

    The coefficients are obtained by integrating over any one period:

    $a_n = {2}/{T} ∫_T f(x) cos(n ω_0 x) dx$,   $b_n = {2}/{T} ∫_T f(x) sin(n ω_0 x) dx$

    and $a_0$ is the one for n = 0, so $a_0/2$ is the mean value of f. With period 2π, $ω_0 = 1$ and the factors are $1/π$.

    Parity

    • If f is even, $f(−x) = f(x)$, all the $b_n$ are 0: cosine series, with $a_n = {4}/{T} ∫_0^{T/2} f(x) cos(n ω_0 x) dx$.
    • If f is odd, $f(−x) = −f(x)$, $a_0$ and the $a_n$ are 0: sine series.

    A function given only on [0, L] can be extended evenly (and expanded in cosines) or oddly (in sines) to [−L, L]; this is what is used to solve the heat equation and the wave equation by separation of variables.

    Convergence and the Gibbs phenomenon

    If f is piecewise differentiable, the series converges to f(x) where f is continuous and, at a jump, to the mean of the one-sided limits. Near a jump the partial sum overshoots the value of f by about 9% of the jump, and that peak does not disappear when more terms are added: it only narrows.

    Parseval's identity

    ${2}/{T} ∫_T f(x)^2 dx = {a_0^2}/{2} + Σ_{n=1}^{∞} (a_n^2 + b_n^2)$

    It says how much “energy” each harmonic carries, and it is useful for summing series: with $f(x) = x$ on [−π, π] you get $Σ 1/n^2 = π^2/6$. The same happens when evaluating the series at a point: the square wave at x = π/2 gives $1 − 1/3 + 1/5 − … = π/4$. To approximate with polynomials instead of sines and cosines use Taylor series.

    Complex form and amplitude-phase form

    With $e^{iθ} = cos θ + i sin θ$ the series is written with exponentials, which is how it is used in signals:

    $f(x) ∼ Σ_{n=−∞}^{∞} c_n e^{i n ω_0 x}$,   $c_n = {1}/{T} ∫_T f(x) e^{−i n ω_0 x} dx = {a_n − i b_n}/{2}$

    and $c_{−n}$ is the conjugate of $c_n$ when f is real. Combining the sine and cosine of each harmonic it also becomes $f(x) ∼ {a_0}/{2} + Σ A_n cos(n ω_0 x + φ_n)$, with amplitude $A_n = √(a_n^2 + b_n^2) = 2|c_n|$ and phase $φ_n$: the $A_n$ are the bars of the spectrum.

    Less simple general terms

    It is not always $1/n$ or $1/n^2$: with $|sin x|$ the denominator is $n^2 − 1$ and the formula holds from n = 2 (the first coefficient is computed separately), with $e^x$ there appear $n^2 + 1$ and sinh π, and in a trapezoidal wave $sin(nπ/2)$ comes out, which goes 1, 0, −1, 0… If the pieces leave a gap, f = 0 is taken there.

    Frequently asked questions

    How are the Fourier coefficients calculated?

    For period T and ω₀ = 2π/T: a₀ = (2/T)∫f dt, aₙ = (2/T)∫f·cos(nω₀t) dt and bₙ = (2/T)∫f·sin(nω₀t) dt, with f = a₀/2 + Σ(aₙ cos nω₀t + bₙ sin nω₀t). The ±1 square wave of period 2π gives aₙ = 0 and bₙ = 4/(nπ) for odd n: f = (4/π)(sin x + sin 3x/3 + sin 5x/5 + …).

    What is the Gibbs phenomenon?

    At a jump, the series converges to the mean of the one-sided limits, but the partial sums overshoot the value by about 9% of the jump, no matter how many harmonics are added. Parseval's identity relates the power of the signal to that of its harmonics.
