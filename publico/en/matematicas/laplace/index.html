    What the Laplace transform is

    It converts a function of time f(t), defined for $t ≥ 0$, into a function F(s) of a variable s:

    $F(s) = ℒ\{f(t)\} = ∫_0^∞ e^{−st} f(t) dt$

    The integral only converges for s greater than a certain value: for example, that of eat exists for s > a. The useful thing is that differentiating in t becomes multiplying by s, so a differential equation turns into an algebraic equation.

    Basic table and properties

    • $ℒ\{1\} = {1}/{s}$,   $ℒ\{t^{n}\} = {n!}/{s^{n+1}}$,   $ℒ\{e^{at}\} = {1}/{s − a}$
    • $ℒ\{sin(bt)\} = {b}/{s^{2} + b^{2}}$,   $ℒ\{cos(bt)\} = {s}/{s^{2} + b^{2}}$
    • $ℒ\{sinh(at)\} = {a}/{s^{2} − a^{2}}$,   $ℒ\{cosh(at)\} = {s}/{s^{2} − a^{2}}$
    • Shift in s: $ℒ\{e^{at} f(t)\} = F(s − a)$
    • Multiplying by t: $ℒ\{t^{n} f(t)\} = (−1)^{n} {d^{n}F}/{ds^{n}}$
    • Derivatives: $ℒ\{y′\} = sY − y(0)$,   $ℒ\{y″\} = s^{2}Y − s y(0) − y′(0)$
    • Products of sines and cosines: they are converted to sums before transforming, $sin^{2} t = {1}/{2} − {1}/{2} cos (2 t)$,   $sin A cos B = {1}/{2} [sin(A + B) + sin(A − B)]$

    Step, delta and piecewise functions

    The unit step $u(t − a)$ (or Heaviside, $H(t − a)$) is 0 before $t = a$ and 1 after. It is used to switch signals on and off, and a piecewise function is written with steps: $f(t) = g_{1}(t) [u(t) − u(t − a)] + g_{2}(t) u(t − a)$.

    • Second shifting theorem: $ℒ\{u(t − a) g(t)\} = e^{−as} ℒ\{g(t + a)\}$, and in particular $ℒ\{u(t − a) f(t − a)\} = e^{−as} F(s)$.
    • Dirac delta: $ℒ\{δ(t − a)\} = e^{−as}$. It is an impulse at $t = a$ of area 1, and $g(t) δ(t − a) = g(a) δ(t − a)$.
    • In the inverse, each $e^{−as}$ delays its part: $ℒ^{−1}\{e^{−as} F(s)\} = u(t − a) f(t − a)$, and a lone constant gives a delta.

    Write piecewise functions as “t if 0 ≤ t < 2; 4 − t if 2 ≤ t < 4; 0 if t ≥ 4”, with the pieces separated by semicolons.

    Inverse by partial fractions

    If F(s) is a quotient of polynomials with the numerator of lower degree, find the roots of the denominator (the poles) and split it into fractions that are in the table:

    • Simple pole p: $A/(s − p)$, with $A = [(s − p) F(s)]_{s = p}$. It gives $A e^{pt}$.
    • Double pole p: $A_1/(s − p) + A_2/(s − p)^{2}$. It gives $(A_1 + A_2 t) e^{pt}$.
    • Complex pair $α ± βi$: $(Bs + C)/((s − α)^{2} + β^{2})$. It gives $e^{αt}$ times a sine and a cosine of βt.

    That is why the poles tell you what f(t) looks like without computing it: to the left of the imaginary axis the terms die out, on it they oscillate forever and to the right they grow.

    Initial and final values

    $f(0^{+}) = lim_{s→∞} s F(s)$,   $lim_{t→∞} f(t) = lim_{s→0} s F(s)$

    The final value one only holds if f(t) tends to something: all the poles of s F(s) to the left of the imaginary axis.

    Differential equations

    For $a y″ + b y′ + c y = g(t)$ with y(0) and y′(0), both sides are transformed, Y(s) is solved for and the inverse is taken. The initial conditions enter on their own, without computing constants at the end. For example, $y″ + y = sin t$ at rest gives $Y(s) = 1/(s^{2} + 1)^{2}$, a double pole at ±i, and the solution $y = {1}/{2} sin t − {1}/{2} t cos t$ grows without bound: that is resonance.

    Transfer function and convolution

    With zero initial conditions, $Y(s) = H(s) G(s)$ with $H(s) = 1/(a s^{2} + b s + c)$. The inverse $h(t)$ is the impulse response, and that of $H(s)/s$, the step response. The product of transforms corresponds to convolution:

    $(f * g)(t) = ∫_{0}^{t} f(τ) g(t − τ) dτ$,   $ℒ\{f * g\} = F(s) G(s)$

    So the response to any input is $y = h * g$: for example, $sin t * cos t = {1}/{2} t sin t$.

    Systems of equations

    For $x′ = a_{11} x + a_{12} y + f(t)$, $y′ = a_{21} x + a_{22} y + g(t)$ both equations are transformed and a linear system in X(s) and Y(s) results, which is solved by Cramer's rule with $Δ(s) = (s − a_{11})(s − a_{22}) − a_{12} a_{21}$. The roots of Δ are the eigenvalues of the system matrix.

    To solve them without Laplace (undetermined coefficients) use Differential equations, and for RC, RL and RLC circuits, Circuit simulator (oscilloscope).

    Frequently asked questions

    What are the basic Laplace transforms?

    L{tⁿ} = n!/sⁿ⁺¹, L{eᵃᵗ} = 1/(s − a), L{sin ωt} = ω/(s² + ω²) and L{cos ωt} = s/(s² + ω²). By linearity, L{3e²ᵗ + sin 4t} = 3/(s − 2) + 4/(s² + 16).

    How is an ODE solved with Laplace?

    Use L{y′} = sY − y(0) and L{y″} = s²Y − s·y(0) − y′(0), solve for Y(s) and invert by partial fractions. For y′ + 2y = 0 with y(0) = 3: sY − 3 + 2Y = 0, Y = 3/(s + 2) and y = 3e⁻²ᵗ.
