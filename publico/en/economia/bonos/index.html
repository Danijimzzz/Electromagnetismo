    Price of a bond

    A bond promises periodic coupons and the repayment of the face value at maturity. Its price is the present value of those cash flows discounted at the return the market demands (the yield):

    $P = ∑_{j=1}^{n} {Q_j}/{(1 + y/m)^j}$, with $Q_j = N·c/m$ and, in the last period, also $N$

    If the coupon is greater than the yield, the bond is worth more than its face value (above par); if smaller, less (below par); if they coincide, it is worth exactly the face value. Price and yield move in opposite directions.

    Macaulay duration

    It is the weighted average time in which the investment is recovered, weighting each cash flow by its present value: $D = ∑ t·PV_t / P$. It is measured in years; it is less than the maturity if there are coupons, and equal to it for a zero-coupon bond.

    Modified duration and interest-rate risk

    $D_m = D/(1 + y/m)$ gives the sensitivity of the price to the yield: $ΔP/P ≈ −D_m·Δy$. The longer the duration, the more the bond falls when rates rise.

    Convexity

    The price-yield relationship is not a line but a convex curve. Convexity $C$ corrects the duration approximation: $ΔP/P ≈ −D_m·Δy + ½·C·Δy²$. With it the estimate gets much closer to the exact price, especially for large changes.

    Frequently asked questions

    How is the price of a bond calculated?

    It is the present value of its cash flows discounted at the yield to maturity. A €1,000 bond with a 5 % coupon over 3 years and a yield of 4 % pays 50, 50 and 1,050: 50/1.04 + 50/1.04² + 1,050/1.04³ ≈ €1,027.75.

    What does a bond's duration measure?

    Macaulay duration is the average time, weighted by present value, in which the investment is recovered: in the example above, about 2.86 years. Divided by (1 + yield) it gives the modified duration, which indicates how much the price falls for each point the yield rises.
