    Ordinary least squares

    The simple regression model is $Y_i = β_0 + β_1 X_i + u_i$, where $u_i$ is the disturbance. The least-squares estimators are those that minimize the sum of squared residuals:

    $β̂_1 = {∑ (x_i − x̄)(y_i − ȳ)}/{∑ (x_i − x̄)^2}$, $β̂_0 = ȳ − β̂_1 x̄$

    $R^2$ measures what part of the variation in Y the model explains, and the test $t = β̂_1/s(β̂_1)$, with $n − 2$ degrees of freedom, says whether the effect of X is significant.

    How to read each form

    In the linear model, $β_1$ is the marginal effect: how much Y changes when X rises by one unit. It is what is used, for example, for the marginal propensity to consume in $C = C_0 + c Y_d$.

    In the double-log model, $ln Y = β_0 + β_1 ln X$, $β_1$ is the elasticity: the percentage change in Y when X rises by 1 %. It is the usual way to estimate demand elasticities or Engel curves.

    In the semi-log model, $ln Y = β_0 + β_1 X$, Y grows at a constant rate: each unit of X multiplies it by $e^{β_1}$. With X in years, $e^{β_1} − 1$ is the annual growth rate.

    Precautions

    Two variables being related does not mean one causes the other: there may be a third behind both, such as time in two series that grow together. Also, predicting outside the range of the data (extrapolating) is risky, and in time series the residuals tend to be correlated, which makes the standard errors come out too small.

    Frequently asked questions

    How is a regression line interpreted in economics?

    The slope is the marginal effect. In the consumption function C = 200 + 0.8Y, each extra euro of income raises consumption by €0.8 (the marginal propensity to consume); R² says what part of the variation income explains.

    And a logarithmic or exponential fit?

    In ln Q = a + b ln P, b is the constant elasticity: with b = −1.5, a 1 % higher price reduces the quantity by 1.5 %. In ln y = a + bt, b is the growth rate.
