    The income statement

    It shows what the company has earned or lost in a year. Starting from sales, supplies, personnel and other operating expenses are subtracted to give EBITDA (the result before depreciation, interest and taxes). Removing depreciation leaves the operating result, what the business itself yields.

    The financial result is added, $"financial income" − "financial expenses"$, giving the EBT, the result before taxes. If it is positive, corporate income tax is paid, $EBT · t$; the rest is the profit for the year. With losses no tax is paid.

    The balance sheet

    It is a snapshot of the company at a date. Assets are what it has: non-current (fixed assets and what stays more than a year) and current (inventories, receivables and cash, which turn into money in less than a year). Equity and liabilities say where the money came from: from the owners (capital, reserves and profit) or from debts, long- or short-term.

    The accounting equation always holds, $"Assets" = "Equity" + "Liabilities"$. Profit for the year is the bridge between the two statements: profit increases equity, and losses reduce it.

    Working capital

    $WC = CA − CL$ is the part of current assets that does not have to be repaid in the short term. If positive, the company covers with what it will collect what it will have to pay; if negative, it may have cash-flow problems. To assess it with ratios, use the Financial ratios tool.

    Frequently asked questions

    What equation does the balance sheet satisfy?

    Assets = Liabilities + Equity. An asset of 500 financed with 300 of debt and 200 of equity balances.

    How do you get from sales to net profit?

    Sales − operating expenses = EBIT; − financial expenses = EBT; − taxes = net profit. With sales 1000, expenses 700, interest 50 and a 25 % tax: EBIT 300, EBT 250 and net profit 187.5.
