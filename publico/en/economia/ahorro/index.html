    Compound interest and contributions

    A capital $C_0$ at an annual return $r$ becomes $C_0 (1 + r)^n$ after $n$ years. If $a$ is also contributed at the end of each period, the contributions form an annuity: its future value is $a · s_{N¬i} = a · {(1 + i)^N − 1}/i$, with $N$ periods at the rate $i$ per period.

    Inflation and purchasing power

    Inflation $π$ is how much prices rise each year. An amount $X$ from $n$ years from now is equivalent today to $X/(1 + π)^n$: it is what can be bought with it at today's prices. With 2 % inflation, prices double in 35 years (rule of 70: $70/2 = 35$).

    Real interest rate

    The Fisher equation separates how much money grows from how much what can be bought grows: $1 + r_{real} = (1 + r)/(1 + π)$, and approximately $r_{real} ≈ r − π$. If the return does not exceed inflation, you are saving while losing purchasing power.

    Planning with a goal

    To have in n years an amount that is worth the same as €X today, you need to gather €X (1 + π)ⁿ of then. What the initial capital does not achieve must be contributed; raising contributions each year with inflation keeps the effort the same in real terms.

    Frequently asked questions

    How much builds up with periodic contributions?

    V = A·((1 + i)ⁿ − 1)/i. With €2,400 a year for 20 years at 3 %: 2,400 · 26.87 ≈ €64,489.

    How does inflation affect it?

    The real rate is (1 + i)/(1 + π) − 1, approximately i − π. With 3 % nominal and 2 % inflation, the real rate is 1.03/1.02 − 1 ≈ 0.98 %.
