Black-Scholes

    What an option is

    A European call gives the right to buy a stock at the strike price K on a future date; a put, to sell it. At expiry the call pays $max(S − K, 0)$ and the put $max(K − S, 0)$. Today it has a price because it may end up worth something: the more volatile the stock and the more time left, the more it is worth.

    The Black-Scholes formula

    $d_1 = {ln(S/K) + (r − q + σ^2/2)T}/{σ√T}$, $d_2 = d_1 − σ√T$

    $C = S e^{−qT} N(d_1) − K e^{−rT} N(d_2)$, $P = K e^{−rT} N(−d_2) − S e^{−qT} N(−d_1)$

    $N$ is the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal. It assumes the price follows a geometric Brownian motion with constant volatility, a constant risk-free rate and markets without costs or arbitrage opportunities. Put-call parity, $C − P = S e^{−qT} − K e^{−rT}$, always holds.

    The Greeks

    Delta is how much the option changes per euro the stock rises (between 0 and 1 for the call, between −1 and 0 for the put). Gamma is how much the delta changes. Vega is the sensitivity to volatility, theta the loss of value as time passes and rho the sensitivity to the interest rate. They are used to hedge the risk of a portfolio of options.

    What this model assumes

    • European option: it can only be exercised at expiry (no early exercise; an American put can be worth more).
    • The stock pays a continuous dividend q (Merton model), not discrete dividends on specific dates.
    • Constant volatility and risk-free rate until expiry, and prices without jumps (geometric Brownian motion).
    • Market with no transaction costs, no taxes and unlimited buying and short selling.
    • Implied volatility is the σ that makes the model give the market price; it is not a forecast, it is a way of expressing that price.
    • Near expiry, gamma and theta can be enormous: that is correct, not an error.

    Limits of this tool

    • It values only European options (exercised at expiry): it does not work for American options with early exercise or for exotic options.
    • It assumes constant volatility and interest rate and a continuous dividend as a percentage: it does not allow discrete dividends on specific dates or a volatility smile.

    Frequently asked questions

    How is the price of a call calculated with Black-Scholes?

    C = S·N(d₁) − K·e^(−rT)·N(d₂), with d₁ = [ln(S/K) + (r + σ²/2)T]/(σ√T) and d₂ = d₁ − σ√T. With S = K = 100, T = 1, r = 5 % and σ = 20 %: d₁ = 0.35, d₂ = 0.15 and the call is worth about €10.45.

    What is the relationship between the price of the call and that of the put?

    Put-call parity: C − P = S − K·e^(−rT). In the previous example, the put is worth 10.45 − (100 − 95.12) ≈ €5.57. With continuous dividends q, S·e^(−qT) is used.
