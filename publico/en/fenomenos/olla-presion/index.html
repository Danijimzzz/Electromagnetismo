The pressure cooker and water in the mountains

At the top of Everest water boils at about 70 °C and there is no way to hard-boil an egg. In a pressure cooker the opposite happens: the water boils at more than 120 °C and lentils are done in a quarter of an hour. It all depends on pressure.

A little history

  1. 1679The French physicist Denis Papin presents in London his "steam digester", a thick-walled pot with a sealed lid that cooked bones until they softened. So that it would not burst, he fitted it with the first safety valve.
  2. 1834Émile Clapeyron, and fifteen years later Rudolf Clausius, explain how the boiling temperature changes with pressure. Their equation is still the one used today.
  3. 19th centuryHimalayan explorers measured the height of mountains with a hypsometer: a thermometer that measures the temperature at which water boils. The higher up, the sooner it boils.
  4. 1919The Spaniard José Alix Martínez patents the "olla exprés", the first pressure cooker designed for the home kitchen.

Which tool it relates to

Calorimetry and phase changes · General physics

Heating the water in the pot is a heat problem: how much energy it takes to raise it from 20 °C to the temperature at which it boils at that pressure, and how long a 2000 W kettle takes to supply it.

Q = m c ΔT = 1 · 4180 · 101 ≈ 422 kJ

t = Q / (η P) = 422 000 / (0,9 · 2000) ≈ 235 s

It opens with 1 kg of water in the cooker (which boils at 121 °C), a 2000 W heater with 90 % efficiency and a final temperature of 121 °C. We get the time it takes to get there, about 3 min 55 s, compared with 3 min 6 s for the open pot: the cooker takes a little longer to heat up, but then cooks four times faster.
