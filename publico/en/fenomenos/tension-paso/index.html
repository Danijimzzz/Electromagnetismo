The step voltage

A high-voltage cable falls to the ground and nobody touches it. Why do they say you should not approach it even on foot, and what should you do if you are caught near it?

A little history

  1. 1785Charles-Augustin de Coulomb publishes that the force between two charges falls with the square of the distance. From that law comes a potential that falls as 1/r, the same one found on the ground around a fallen cable.
  2. 1827Georg Simon Ohm publishes that the current through a conductor is proportional to the voltage across its ends: V = I·R. With it we calculate the current that flows through the body.
  3. 20th centuryCharles Dalziel, at the University of California, Berkeley, measures in volunteers which currents can be endured and for how long. From his data come the step and touch voltages allowed by the earthing standards for substations.
  4. 2014In Spain, the Regulation on high-voltage electrical installations (RD 337/2014) requires in its ITC-RAT 13 that earthing systems keep step and touch voltages below certain safe limits.
E

Which tool it relates to

Electrostatics · Electromagnetism

Homogeneous ground that conducts current behaves, mathematically, like a vacuum with a charge: the current I entering at the contact point takes the place of the charge and the resistivity ρ, that of 1/ε. The potential is that of a point charge, and the step voltage is the potential difference between the two feet.

V(r) = {ρ I}/{2π r} , ΔV = V(r) − V(r + d)

I_{body} = {ΔV}/{R_{body} + 6ρ}

It opens with a point charge of 1.77 µC (which satisfies k·q = ρI/2π = 15.9 kV·m) in SI. We get a potential difference of 8.0 kV between the feet at 1 m and 2 m from the contact, 145 V between 10 m and 11 m, and a field of 15.9 kV/m at 1 m.
