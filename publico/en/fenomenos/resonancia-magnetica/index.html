Magnetic resonance imaging

You lie down in a noisy tunnel, with no X-rays or ionising radiation, and a detailed image of your knee or your brain comes out. Behind it there is a giant magnet, radio waves and the frequency at which the protons of your water spin.

A little history

  1. 1938Isidor Rabi measures the magnetic resonance of nuclei in a beam of molecules: it is the first nuclear magnetic resonance experiment. He receives the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1944.
  2. 1946Felix Bloch and Edward Purcell, separately, detect resonance in liquids and solids. They share the 1952 Nobel Prize in Physics.
  3. 1973Paul Lauterbur proposes using field gradients to locate the signal and obtains the first image; Peter Mansfield improves the technique. Both receive the Nobel Prize in Medicine in 2003.
  4. 1977Raymond Damadian and his team obtain the first image of an entire human body, after many hours of scanning.
  5. Since 1980Clinical scanners spread through hospitals: today 1.5 and 3 T ones are common, and there are 7 T ones for research.

Which tool it relates to

Magnetostatics (Biot-Savart) · Electromagnetism

The scanner's magnet is an enormous solenoid: its field at the centre is given by Ampère's law, and from it comes the frequency at which the radio pulse has to be emitted.

B₀ = μ₀ n I = μ₀ (N / L) I ≈ 1,500 T

f = (γ / 2π) B₀ = 42.577 MHz/T · B₀ ≈ 63.87 MHz

It opens with an ideal solenoid 2 m long and 0.4 m in radius, 4775 turns and 500 A (in SI mode), with the question "field B at the centre". You can change the current or the turns and see how the field changes and, with it, the Larmor frequency. The data are indicative: a real magnet is superconducting and made of several coils.
