    These are from the IEC 60062 standard. The digits run from 0 to 9 in the order black, brown, red, orange, yellow, green, blue, violet, grey and white: between black and white, the colours of the rainbow.

    How a resistor is read

    • 4 bands (the most common, carbon, ±5 %): two digits, the multiplier and the tolerance. Yellow, violet, red and gold are 47 × 100 = 4700 Ω = 4.7 kΩ ±5 %.
    • 5 bands (metal film, ±1 %): three digits, the multiplier and the tolerance.
    • 6 bands: like the 5-band ones plus one more for the temperature coefficient.
    • 3 bands: two digits and the multiplier, with no tolerance band, which is then ±20 %.

    You start from the end opposite the tolerance band, which is usually set slightly apart from the others. Gold and silver never come first, and the first band is never black.

    $R = (digits) · 10^n$,   $R_{min} = R (1 − t)$,   $R_{max} = R (1 + t)$

    Standard values: the E series

    Resistors are not made in arbitrary values but in those of the E series. The E12 has 12 values per decade, each about $10^{1/12}$ = 1.21 times the previous one, so that the tolerance ranges (±10 %) almost touch. The E24 goes with ±5 %, the E48 with ±2 % and the E96 with ±1 %.

    E12: 10, 12, 15, 18, 22, 27, 33, 39, 47, 56, 68 and 82, multiplied by the appropriate power of 10 (1.2 Ω, 120 Ω, 12 kΩ…).

    SMD resistors

    Surface-mount ones have the value printed on them. With 3 digits (5 %), the first two are the digits and the third the number of zeros: 472 = 47 × 10² = 4.7 kΩ. With 4 digits (1 %) they are three digits and the zeros: 1002 = 10 kΩ. The R acts as the decimal point: 4R7 = 4.7 Ω. In the EIA-96 code, two digits give the position in the E96 series and a letter the multiplier: 01C = 100 × 100 = 10 kΩ.

    Inductors and capacitors

    Small inductors use the same colours as resistors, with the value in microhenries: brown, black, black and silver are 10 μH ±10 %. Ceramic capacitors have a 3-digit code in picofarads: 104 = 10 × 104 pF = 100 nF. A tolerance letter (J ±5 %, K ±10 %, M ±20 %) and a maximum voltage code (1H = 50 V, 2A = 100 V) may follow. The prefix letter is also written in place of the decimal point: 4n7 = 4.7 nF.

    Series, parallel and values that do not exist

    In series resistances add, $R = R_1 + R_2$; in parallel the inverses add, $1/R = 1/R_1 + 1/R_2$, which for two is the product over the sum. This is how a value not in the series is obtained: 5 kΩ is not in the E12, but two 10 kΩ in parallel give it exactly.

    LED and voltage divider

    An LED has to lose the voltage that does not drop across it, and the resistor limits it: $R = (V_s − V_{LED})/I$, rounding up to the next standard value so as not to exceed the current. In a divider, $V_{out} = V_{in} R_2/(R_1 + R_2)$; if a load is connected, it sits in parallel with R2 and the output drops, unless the load is much larger than R2.

    Frequently asked questions

    How is the colour code of a resistor read?

    The first two bands are digits, the third the multiplier and the fourth the tolerance. Brown-black-red-gold is 10 × 10² = 1 kΩ with ±5 %.

    How is the resistance of a combination calculated?

    In series, R = R₁ + R₂ + …; in parallel, 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + …. Two 100 Ω resistors in parallel give 50 Ω, and a 330 Ω and a 470 Ω in series give 800 Ω.
