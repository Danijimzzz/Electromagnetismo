    Does this field satisfy the equations?

    To find out whether a pair $E(x, y, z, t)$ and $B(x, y, z, t)$ can be an electromagnetic field you have to check the four equations in differential form: $∇·E = ρ/ε_0$, $∇·B = 0$, $∇×E = −∂B/∂t$ and $∇×B = μ_0 J + μ_0 ε_0 ∂E/∂t$. The «Does this field satisfy the equations?» calculator differentiates exactly what you type, evaluates the difference between both sides at several points and tells you which one fails. A plane wave $E_y = E_0 cos(ωt − kz)$ satisfies Faraday and Ampère–Maxwell only if $B_x = −E_0/c · cos(ωt − kz)$ and $ω = ck$.

    The four equations

    All of classical electromagnetism fits in four equations and the Lorentz force. Each one is written in integral form (over a surface or a curve, the one used in problems with symmetry) and in differential form (at each point), and both say the same thing. Click one to open its calculator.

    Gauss's law for E · charges create the electric field

    $∮ E·dS = Q_{enc}/ε_0$

    $∇·E = ρ/ε_0$

    The flux of E through a closed surface depends only on the charge it encloses. It is used to calculate the fields of spheres, wires and planes.

    ElectrostaticsCapacitors

    Gauss's law for B · there are no monopoles

    $∮ B·dS = 0$

    $∇·B = 0$

    Magnetic field lines are closed: whatever enters a closed surface leaves it.

    Magnetostatics

    Faraday's law · a changing B creates an E

    $∮ E·dl = −{dΦ_B}/{dt}$

    $∇ × E = −{∂B}/{∂t}$

    The emf induced in a loop is minus the derivative of the flux (Lenz's law). It is the principle of the generator and the transformer.

    Faraday's lawInduction

    Ampère–Maxwell law · currents and a changing E create B

    $∮ B·dl = μ_0 I_{enc} + μ_0 ε_0 {dΦ_E}/{dt}$

    $∇ × B = μ_0 J + μ_0 ε_0 {∂E}/{∂t}$

    Maxwell added the last term, the displacement current, so that the law also holds between the plates of a capacitor. With it, waves appear.

    MagnetostaticsCapacitorsEM waves

    Consequence: charge is conserved · ∇·J = −∂ρ/∂t

    $∇·J + {∂ρ}/{∂t} = 0$

    $ρ(t) = ρ_0 e^{−t/τ}$,   $τ = ε/σ$

    It comes from the divergence of Ampère–Maxwell. In an ohmic medium the excess free charge dissipates with τ = ε/σ: in copper, 10⁻¹⁹ s; in distilled water, tenths of a millisecond.

    CapacitorsCircuits

    The result: light is a wave · c = 1/√(μ₀ε₀)

    $∇² E = μ_0 ε_0 {∂²E}/{∂t²}$

    $c = 1/√{μ_0 ε_0} ≈ 3·10^8 "m/s"$

    In vacuum, Faraday and Ampère–Maxwell chain together: a changing E creates a changing B, which creates an E… and it propagates at the speed of light.

    Integral and differential: two theorems

    You go from one form to the other with two theorems of vector calculus. The divergence theorem, $∮ A·dS = ∫ ∇·A dV$, turns the fluxes through closed surfaces (the two Gauss laws) into divergences. Stokes' theorem, $∮ A·dl = ∫ (∇ × A)·dS$, turns the circulations (Faraday and Ampère–Maxwell) into curls. That is why the two forms are equivalent; the differential one describes each point and the integral one is convenient when there is symmetry.

    Charge is conserved

    If you take the divergence of the Ampère–Maxwell law and use Gauss's law, you get the continuity equation:

    $∇·J + {∂ρ}/{∂t} = 0$

    Without the displacement current this equation would not come out and charge would not be conserved. That is the reason Maxwell had to add it.

    In matter

    Inside a dielectric or a magnet it is convenient to put the charges and currents of matter into two new fields, $D = ε_0 E + P$ and $H = B/μ_0 − M$. Then the equations that involve only free sources are $∇·D = ρ_{free}$ and $∇ × H = J_{free} + {∂D}/{∂t}$. In a linear medium, $D = εE$ and $B = μH$, and the speed of waves is $v = 1/√{με}$.

    The Lorentz force

    The equations say how the fields are created; how they act on a charge is given by the Lorentz force:

    $F = q (E + v × B)$

    Together, the four equations and this force explain everything the course covers, from Coulomb's law to radio waves.

    Frequently asked questions

    What are Maxwell's equations?

    In differential form: ∇·E = ρ/ε₀ (Gauss), ∇·B = 0 (no magnetic monopoles), ∇×E = −∂B/∂t (Faraday) and ∇×B = μ₀J + μ₀ε₀ ∂E/∂t (Ampère-Maxwell).

    How do you go from the integral form to the differential form?

    With the divergence and Stokes theorems: ∮E·dS = Q/ε₀ becomes ∇·E = ρ/ε₀, and ∮E·dl = −dΦ_B/dt becomes ∇×E = −∂B/∂t. From the four of them comes the speed of electromagnetic waves, 1/√(μ₀ε₀) = c.
