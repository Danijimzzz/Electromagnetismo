Self-inductance and mutual inductance

Calculate the self-inductance coefficient L of solenoids, toroids, coaxial cables and twin-wire lines, toroids with an air gap, the mutual inductance M between two circuits and of coupled coils in series or in parallel, the magnetic energy and the induced emf, and follow the transient of an RL circuit, also with the source being switched on and off.

    Self-inductance

    A current I creates a field B and, with it, a flux through the circuit itself. That flux is proportional to the current, and the constant is the self-inductance coefficient, which only depends on the shape of the circuit and the medium. It is measured in henries (H = Wb/A).

    $Φ = L I$,   $ε = −L {dI}/{dt}$,   $U = 1/2 L I^2 = ∫ B^2/(2μ) dV$

    To calculate it: find B with Ampère's law assuming a current I, integrate the flux through one turn and multiply by the number of turns. In a long solenoid L = μ0 N2 S / l; in a toroid of rectangular cross-section, L = μ0 N2 h ln(b/a) / (2π).

    Magnetic circuits and air gap

    $ℛ = l/{μ S}$,   $Φ = {N I}/{ℛ_n + ℛ_g}$,   $L = N^2/ℛ$

    A core with an air gap is like two resistors in series: the reluctance of the gap, g/(μ0S), usually weighs more than that of the whole core, because μr is very large. That is why an air gap of a few millimetres greatly reduces L, but makes it almost independent of the material.

    Mutual inductance

    If the current of one circuit creates flux in another, Φ21 = M I1 and a varying current induces in the second ε2 = −M dI1/dt. The coefficient is the same in both directions (M12 = M21), so it is convenient to calculate it with the circuit whose field is easy. The coupling coefficient k = M / √(L1 L2) goes from 0 to 1; it is 1 when all the flux of one passes through the other.

    $L_{series} = L_1 + L_2 ± 2M$,   $L_{parallel} = {L_1 L_2 − M^2}/{L_1 + L_2 ∓ 2M}$

    The upper sign holds when the fluxes of the two coils add, and the lower one when they oppose. For two coaxial loops a distance z apart, if the small one is small, M = μ0π N1N2 R2 r2 / (2(R2 + z2)3/2); for a coil inside a solenoid, M = μ0 N1 N2 π r2 cos θ / l.

    Force between loops

    The interaction energy of two circuits with fixed currents is $U = M I_1 I_2$ and the force between them $F = I_1 I_2 {dM}/{dz}$. Currents in the same direction attract and opposite ones repel. For two coaxial loops M is calculated exactly with elliptic integrals; if the small one is much smaller than the distance, it behaves like a dipole and $F ≈ −{3 μ_0 π N_1 N_2 I_1 I_2 R^2 r^2 z}/{2(R^2 + z^2)^{5/2}}$, which at large distance falls as $1/z^4$.

    Transformer with real coupling

    Two coils with $L_2 = L_1 (N_2/N_1)^2$ and $M = k √(L_1 L_2)$. In sinusoidal steady state, with phasors: $V_1 = (R_1 + jωL_1) I_1 − jωM I_2$ and $jωM I_1 = (R_2 + R_L + jωL_2) I_2$. With $k = 1$, zero resistances and large $L_1$ the ideal transformer comes out, $V_2/V_1 = N_2/N_1$. With $k < 1$ the secondary voltage drops under load (regulation $= (V_{20} − V_2)/V_{20}$) and the efficiency $η = P_2/P_1$ falls because of the copper and the flux that leaks. To see the street-pole transformer step by step, look at The pole transformer.

    RL circuit

    The coil opposes changes in current: when the circuit is closed the current does not jump to ε/R, but grows with the time constant τ = L/R, and when the source is removed it decays with that same τ while the coil returns its energy, which is dissipated in the resistance.

    $I(t) = ε/R (1 − e^{−t/τ})$,   $I(t) = I_0 e^{−t/τ}$,   $τ = L/R$

    While charging, the source supplies $W_ε = ε I_0 (t − τ(1 − e^{−t/τ}))$: part stays in the coil, $1/2 L I^2$, and the rest is dissipated in R. While discharging, all the energy of the coil is dissipated. If the source is switched on and off again and again, the current ends up oscillating between a fixed maximum and minimum, with a mean of $ε/R · t_{on}/T$.

    For the emf that appears when the flux changes (moving bars, rotating loops, fields that change with time) use Faraday and Lenz.

    Frequently asked questions

    How is the self-inductance of a solenoid calculated?

    L = μ₀N²A/ℓ. For example, with N = 500 turns, A = 4 cm² and ℓ = 20 cm: L = 4π·10⁻⁷ · 500² · 4·10⁻⁴ / 0.2 ≈ 0.63 mH. The stored energy is U = ½LI².

    How does an RL circuit behave?

    The current grows as i(t) = (V/R)(1 − e^(−t/τ)), with time constant τ = L/R. With L = 10 mH and R = 5 Ω, τ = 2 ms and the current reaches 63 % of V/R in that time.
